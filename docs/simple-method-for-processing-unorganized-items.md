# A Simple Method for Processing Unorganized Items

Work through a collection one item or group at a time. Choose what to do,
carry out the actions, and check the results.

The **collection** is where eligible items come from. A **scope** is the item or
group you are considering now. **Action targets** are the items an operation
affects. These can differ: an inbox may be the collection, 20 related booking
emails the scope, and different subsets the targets of deleting or archiving.

## Before you start

- Choose the collection, purpose and work limit.
- Define the included items and the content or state you will work on.
  Collections may be nested: specify which containers and contents are included,
  and how far into the hierarchy you will work.
- Set the order for choosing items, including ties and selection across included
  levels. By default, order the eligible items you have discovered. If order
  across the whole collection matters, require evidence that establishes it,
  such as a reliable sorted view. With incomplete discovery, the newest item
  found is not necessarily the newest in the collection; do not silently relax
  a required global order.

Exclude later arrivals unless explicitly included. Say if you have not found all
items in the collection. Defining its boundary does not require a complete map
or inspection of every item or descendant before starting.

## Work through each scope

Inspect items or gather context whenever useful for choosing members or deciding
what to do. Existing evidence may be enough. Judge further work by the improvement
it could make to the decision, its time or resource cost, the consequences of an
error, and its usefulness for later work. A quick reference search may settle a
grouping question; a costly comparison may be worthwhile before deleting a file
that may be unique or when its findings will help with later items. Item count
alone does not decide how much investigation is worthwhile.

### Choose the scope

Pick the first unprocessed item in the chosen order, skipping blocked items
without losing track of them. This starts the scope; grouped items need not share
the starting item's age or position in that order.

A scope may include a container, selected contents, or both.

One item can be a useful scope. You may expand it to a group, including the whole
collection. To find candidates, you might browse, search for a shared reference,
topic, person or date, compare contents, follow links, or ask for relevant context.
A search through nested folders may yield a scope of a few matching files.

Set clear boundaries and define:

- how the members are related for this purpose;
- what evidence confirms that relationship;
- which exceptions exclude items.

Search results or similarity suggest candidates; confirm membership against the
purpose, evidence and exceptions. Include only matches whose handling you will
decide together. Leave uncertain membership pending. Keep other relevant material
as context. For example, a reference library can help assess a downloaded file
without becoming part of the scope or authorizing changes to that library.

Prefer refining the scope toward **one appropriate shared handling action**, such
as archiving related emails. Treat that action as provisional; do not make the
evidence fit it. A smaller scope may help when splitting does not hide dependencies
or add disproportionate work. Keep items together when joint consideration or a
shared result matters, even if their final handling needs several actions. One
item may also need several actions. The preference for one handling action does
not limit supporting searches or checks.

An optional scope view can show the selected items and relevant context.

### Decide what to do

Make or obtain an authorized **handling decision** that answers:

- What should happen, and to which targets?
- What conditions must hold?
- What result would satisfy the decision?

Existing policy may supply the decision.

When a recommendation is useful, make the proposed action clear and show known
exceptions or necessary combinations. The final decision may change the action
or targets, or use several actions on different parts of the same scope. For the
20 booking emails, it might be to delete 15 and archive 5; that change alone does
not require splitting the scope or restarting.

### Act and check

For each **action**, specify the operation and its targets. A decision may require
several actions to gather information or change state. Act within your authority.
For actions on containers, make any effects on their contents explicit.

When approval is required, show the final actions, targets, effects and conditions
for review. Obtain approval before execution, individually or as a set; approval
must cover the actions actually being taken.

Act while the decision's conditions still hold. Compare **results**—actual effects
or findings, including uncertainty—with the decision. Revisit the scope or
decision if material information changes.

Keep decisions, results and unfinished work recoverable as you go.

Mark an item or group **processed** when its chosen handling is achieved and you can
verify it from evidence. A summary or leaving something unchanged can satisfy the
decision. If handling depends on a shared result, leave it unfinished until that
result is confirmed.

Count included contents as processed only when the decision for them has been
fulfilled and checked. One action on a container can be enough if it achieves
that result for all included contents; it does not establish that every item
was individually examined.

## If work remains

Find out what actually happened before retrying an uncertain action; reuse
confirmed successes. If blocked, continue only with independent work or an
authorized next step.

Arranging follow-up work can complete processing when that is the chosen handling.
Specify the next action, who is responsible, where the information is accessible,
and how the work will reliably be picked up or reconsidered. Confirm that the receiving
workflow accepts this kind of work and preserves the information and conditions
needed to continue. Existing intake arrangements may suffice; a recorded entry
alone does not.

## Continue or stop

Return to the next unprocessed item. Stop when all items are processed, the work
limit is reached or no authorized work can proceed.

Report the collection boundary, coverage, processed items and unfinished or shared
obligations. Reporting zero unprocessed items means none remain in the defined
collection; it does not mean the source is empty or follow-up work is complete.
If you have not found all items, do not claim zero for the whole collection.
